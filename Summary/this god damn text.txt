Idea 1: CounterSAR, “What should the radar have seen?”

Problem statement: Change detection fails because it compares two noisy images. Instead of comparing image A to image B, predict what a pixel should look like on a given day if nothing unusual happened, then flag deviations from that expectation.

Approach:

Build a conditional generative model (diffusion model or transformer) that predicts expected L-band backscatter for any location and date. Inputs: DEM, land cover, season, recent rainfall, soil moisture (SMAP), temperature (ERA5), and the viewing geometry (incidence angle, orbit direction).
Train it self-supervised on years of NISAR/Sentinel-1 time series, so it learns normal behavior: monsoon darkening, crop cycles, wet soil.
For a new acquisition, compute the surprise residual (observed minus expected), with conformal prediction to give calibrated uncertainty.
Output a surprise map with a confidence value per pixel, plus a reason tag (e.g., “dark but consistent with seasonal wetness, not flagged”).

Why it’s novel: A flooded field and a wet field both look dark. CounterSAR knows the field was expected to be wet this week, so it only alerts on what is truly unexpected. It also removes the need for labeled flood data.

Idea 2: Noise as Signal, turning NISAR’s artifacts into a free sensor

Problem statement: The field treats ionospheric distortion and radio-frequency interference (RFI) as nuisances to remove. But they are physical measurements of the ionosphere and of the radio environment. L-band radar sees them more strongly than most instruments, so they can be mapped as a new data product instead of discarded.

Approach:

Use a blind source separation network (e.g., a U-Net or VAE with physics-based loss terms) to split each interferogram into three parts: ground deformation, atmospheric/ionospheric phase, and RFI/processing artifacts.
Train the artifact branch using synthetic data: inject simulated ionospheric screens and RFI into clean interferograms so you have ground truth.
Convert the extracted ionospheric component into maps of ionospheric irregularities (TEC gradients, scintillation-prone regions) and validate against GNSS-derived TEC data.
Build an RFI source map that clusters recurring interference to locate likely terrestrial emitters.
Bonus: use the cleaned deformation output, which also improves InSAR, so you deliver both a better result and a new product.

Why it’s novel: The pitch is “we turn NISAR’s biggest weakness into a byproduct dataset for space-weather and spectrum-monitoring communities.” That story works well for NASA judges.

Risks: Real ground truth is limited, so lean on synthetic data plus GNSS validation. Be honest that the ionospheric component is only recoverable when the signal is strong enough.

NASA is the space agency of the United States, and ISRO is India's space agency.

### 2. What is SAR?

Imagine taking a photograph of Earth from space.

A normal camera uses light to capture images. But what happens when there are clouds, darkness or heavy rain?

This is where SAR comes in.

SAR stands for Synthetic Aperture Radar.

Instead of taking normal photographs, it sends radar waves towards Earth and studies the signals that bounce back.

Think of it like an echo. A bat uses echoes to understand its surroundings; SAR uses reflected radar signals to understand Earth's surface.

The interesting thing is that SAR can observe Earth during the day, at night and through clouds.

### 3. But here is the actual problem!

Imagine a farmer has a field in Karnataka.

During summer, the field is dry. During monsoon, the soil becomes wet. The radar notices changes in the signals reflected from that field.

Now imagine heavy flooding happens.

The radar again notices a change.

*Here is the problem: How does the satellite know whether the change is normal seasonal wetness or an actual flood?*

Both situations might produce similar-looking radar images.

This creates false alarms.

For example:
- A normal wet field might be incorrectly identified as a flood.
- Seasonal changes might be mistaken for disasters.
- Actual unusual changes might go unnoticed among all the normal changes.

Traditional change detection often compares two satellite images and looks for differences.

But not every difference means something bad has happened.

### 4. OUR IDEA: CounterSAR

*What if instead of simply comparing two images, we teach AI what the Earth normally looks like?*

That's exactly what CounterSAR proposes.

Imagine teaching a child what normal weather looks like throughout the year.

After observing several summers and monsoons, the child understands what is normal.

Now, if something unusual happens, the child can recognise it.

We want to teach AI the same thing about Earth's surface.

CounterSAR will learn:
- How soil normally changes during monsoon.
- How agricultural fields change across seasons.
- How rainfall affects radar signals.
- How vegetation and soil moisture affect the radar.
- What normal conditions look like for a particular location and date.

When a new satellite image arrives, the AI will predict what the radar SHOULD have observed if everything were normal.

It then compares:

*What AI expected vs What the satellite actually observed.*

If the difference is unusually large, the system flags it as a possible unexpected event.

# CounterSAR: the What, Why, and How

## WHAT are we building?

**One sentence:** a system that predicts what a radar satellite *should* see at a place on a given date, given the weather, and raises an alert only when the real image can't be explained by that prediction.

The pieces:
- **Input:** radar images (SAR) of a region over many years, plus rainfall, soil moisture, terrain, and land type.
- **Brain:** a model trained on the past to learn "normal for this place, this season, this weather."
- **Output:** a green/yellow/red map, a chart showing *why* each location got its colour, and a confidence level.

**What it is not:** another flood-detection model. It is an *"is this unexpected?"* detector. Flood is the first use case because it's the clearest demo.

## WHY are we doing this?

Here is the problem as a chain:

1. SAR sees through clouds and darkness, so it's the best tool for monsoon-season monitoring, when optical satellites are blind.
2. But radar signals change for many reasons: rain, soil wetness, crop growth, harvest, flooding.
3. Traditional change detection just compares two images, so it flags *every* difference.
4. The result is many false alarms (normal monsoon, flooded paddy fields) mixed in with real events.
5. So analysts either drown in alerts or stop trusting the system. Real disasters get lost in the noise.

**Why our approach helps:**
- If the model already knows what rain, season, and land type normally do to the signal, it can subtract that out.
- What remains is the *unexplained* part, and that is far more likely to be a real event.
- Fewer false alarms means alerts are trusted, which means faster response.

**Why it suits a hackathon:**
- We can train on historical data, so we don't need hand-labelled flood images.
- There's a clear measurable claim: *fewer false alarms at the same detection rate.*
- It ties into the NASA-ISRO NISAR mission, which gives it a strong real-world story.

## HOW are we doing it?

### Step 1: Pick a focused region and test events
- Cause: a broad region means shallow results and no clear story.
- So we choose **one region** (Kuttanad in Kerala is a strong candidate) where paddy is flooded on purpose every season. That's the hardest false alarm for ordinary detectors.
- We also pick known real events (e.g. the 2018 Kerala floods) to test against.

### Step 2: Collect data
- **SAR images:** Sentinel-1 (NISAR as a stretch goal, pending data access).
- **Weather and land:** ERA5 rainfall and temperature, SMAP soil moisture, elevation, and land cover.
- Google Earth Engine can supply most of these, which saves days of downloading.

### Step 3: Train the "what's normal" model
- Input: location, date, rainfall, soil moisture, land type.
- Target: the radar value that was actually observed in past normal years.
- Start simple (quantile regression or gradient boosting), because it also gives us uncertainty. Deep models are an optional upgrade.
- Effect: the model learns, for example, "heavy rain in July on paddy land means this brightness range."

### Step 4: Compare expected vs actual
- A new image arrives and the model predicts the expected radar value.
- Gap = actual − expected.
- Our key trick is **counterfactual weather**: run the model once with the real rainfall and once with normal rainfall. That splits the change into *seasonal part + weather part + unexplained part*, and only the unexplained part can trigger an alert.

### Step 5: Decide when to alert, with a guarantee
- Conformal prediction sets the threshold using past data.
- Effect: we can state a calibrated false-alarm rate (e.g. 5%) instead of guessing a cutoff.

### Step 6: Show and explain
- Streamlit app with a Folium map for the green/yellow/red view.
- Per-location chart of the three-part breakdown, with a short plain-language explanation of why it was flagged.

### Step 7: Prove it works
- Compare against baselines: simple image difference, a threshold method, and a seasonal-only model with no weather.
- Replay the real flood and a normal-monsoon year.
- The proof we want is **flood flagged, normal monsoon stays green, and fewer false alarms than the baselines.**

## The whole story in one chain

Radar changes for many reasons → simple comparison flags all of them → too many false alarms → so we teach AI what's *normal given the weather* → subtract it → only the unexplained change remains → calibrate the threshold → alerts become fewer and more trustworthy.

# Research gap for CounterSAR

## First, a reality check

I searched the literature, and **"predict what the radar should have seen, then compare" has already been done.** If you pitch that as the novelty, a judge who knows the area will catch it. Here is what already exists:

- A 2026 ISPRS paper builds a ConvLSTM that predicts a normal-state SAR image from earlier time-series behaviour, then compares it with the actual flood-time image using a Siamese network. It reports fewer false detections than conventional methods, specifically from seasonal vegetation change and speckle.
- A December 2025 paper does temporal inpainting on satellite time series, learning to predict what the surface should look like without change. That one is on optical imagery, but the idea is the same.
- Autoencoders trained on Sentinel-1 time series have been used to learn the expected temporal signature of undisturbed forest and flag deviations.
- RNNs have been used on InSAR coherence time series as probabilistic anomaly detectors for damage mapping.

**So your gap is not "expected vs observed."** It has to be in what *conditions* the expectation and what you do with the deviation.

## The real gaps (each as a cause → effect chain)

### Gap 1: Existing "expected" models learn from past radar alone, not from weather
- From the excerpts I read, the ConvLSTM approach predicts from earlier SAR behaviour only.
- Cause: a model that sees only past radar can't know that *this* July had unusually heavy rain.
- Effect: a rain-driven change looks like an anomaly, which is a false alarm.
- Weather-related surface conditions such as intercepted rain are known to affect Sentinel-1 backscatter. So the physics supports conditioning on weather.
- **CounterSAR fills this** by feeding rainfall and soil moisture into the expectation model.

### Gap 2: Nobody (that I found) separates the change into weather-explained vs unexplained
- Existing methods output "anomaly or not."
- Our counterfactual decomposition splits the deviation into *seasonal + weather-explained + unexplained*.
- Effect: the output says *why* a location was flagged, which analysts can act on.
- **I did not find a paper doing exactly this.** That is not proof none exists, so say "to our knowledge."

### Gap 3: Calibrated alert thresholds are rare in this "expected vs observed" setup
- A 2024 EUSIPCO paper uses conformal prediction for unsupervised SAR change detection with a controlled false-alarm probability, but on pairs of TerraSAR-X images.
- Conformal methods have also been used to quantify uncertainty in SAR flood maps.
- Cause: those apply conformal prediction to image pairs or flood maps, not to the residual of a weather-conditioned seasonal forecast.
- **CounterSAR fills this** by calibrating the threshold on the forecast residual.

### Gap 4: The monsoon-paddy false alarm is documented but not solved in this framing
- After the 2018 Western Japan floods, change detection misclassified many agricultural fields. The authors attributed this to using a single image pair, ignoring how backscatter in farmland changes over time, and the effect of wavelength.
- Work on Indian rice fields has used Sentinel-1 backscatter with local thresholds to identify flooding at different crop stages. That is threshold-based mapping, not weather-conditioned anomaly detection.
- Rice-paddy flood extraction with Sentinel-1 has also been done in Indonesia using a backscatter threshold with a paddy mask.
- Cause: tropical, monsoon-driven paddy regions are where seasonal flooding looks most like disaster flooding.
- **CounterSAR fills this** with a focused case study on Kerala or Karnataka paddy land.

> **CounterSAR asks a better question: not "what changed?" but "what should have happened, what actually happened, and what neither the season nor the weather can explain?"**

## The problem

Radar sees through clouds and monsoon, which makes it ideal for flood and wetland monitoring. But backscatter swings naturally with rainfall, soil moisture, crop cycles, and water management. Image-to-image change detection can't separate ordinary rhythm from abnormal events, so alerts cry wolf and non-experts can't interpret them.

## The solution

CounterSAR learns what a place should look like and checks every new radar scene against **two expectations**:

| Seasonal expectation (usual for this date?) | Weather-aware expectation (usual given this year's rain, soil moisture, crop phase?) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | Normal | **Expected** |
| Unusual | Normal | **Unusual but explained** (a heavy monsoon filled the fields) |
| Unusual | Unusual | **Unexplained**, the candidate event worth a human look |

That third class is the actionable one. Plain change detection can't produce it.

## How it works

- **Learns "normal"** from a decade of Sentinel-1 history over Kuttanad, with ERA5 rainfall, SMAP soil moisture, Copernicus DEM, WorldCover, and a paddy crop-calendar layer.
- **Predicts expected backscatter with uncertainty** using a model ladder: seasonal climatology, then quantile gradient boosting, then a small spatiotemporal network only if it earns its place.
- **Scores deviations** as z = (R_obs − R_pred) / (σ_pred + ε), requires persistence across consecutive acquisitions, and calibrates uncertainty so "confidence" means something.
- **Runs on real NISAR data.** NISAR L-band scenes are the "now" layer on top of the long Sentinel-1 record. We state openly that NISAR's own seasonal archive is still short, so the baseline is sensor-transferred and calibrated.
- **Explains with an evidence ledger.** Every finding separates *Observed*, *Contextual*, and *Hypothesis*. Radar differences never get presented as proven causes.

## What the judges can touch (interactive app)

1. **Story Mode:** a guided scroll through Kuttanad: the normal monsoon rhythm, the 2018 flood, and NISAR's view now, each step with a map swipe and a plain-language caption.
2. **Expected vs Actual swipe:** observed, predicted, anomaly, and uncertainty layers.
3. **Polarization and Frequency Explorer:** toggle HH, HV, and ratios in L-band, compare with C-band, and see why L-band penetrates vegetation. We report only what our data shows.
4. **Time Machine:** animated multi-date history with NISAR scenes slotted in.
5. **Anomaly card:** verdict, confidence, estimated affected hectares with error bars, physical-driver panel, and a "what we don't see" note for places where radar stays quiet despite an expected signal.
6. **Educator mode:** glossary, "spot the flood" activity, and printable one-page summaries, for teachers and the public.

## Proof it works (pre-registered)

| Test | Success target (fixed before the final run) |
|---|---|
| Prediction on held-out years | At least 15% lower error than seasonal climatology |
| False alarms at equal recall | At least 30% fewer than image differencing |
| Event validation | Verdicts on the 2018 Kerala flood agree with independent flood-extent labels and rainfall records |
| Polarimetry | L-band HH/HV features improve flooded-vegetation F1 over backscatter alone |
| Usability | Non-experts correctly answer "is this unusual?" in a small user test |

Targets are placeholders until your pilot run; set them, then don't move them. If a target is missed, we report it. A result that doesn't clear its bar still counts as an honest finding, and judges trust it more than a spotless one.

## Why it fits the challenge

- **Tracks real surface change with NISAR:** floods and wetland dynamics at multiple time steps.
- **Hypothesizes physical drivers:** rainfall, moisture, crop cycles, water management, and the polarizations that reveal them.
- **Tells a story for a broad audience:** Story Mode, swipes, animation, and educator tools.
- **Open source:** public repo, documentation, and an object-oriented, swappable architecture (data sources, expectation models, interpreters) that extends to forests, glaciers, or ground deformation by adding one class.
- **Looks ahead:** as NISAR's multi-year archive builds, the baseline becomes NISAR-native, and InSAR products can extend the same framework to subsidence and earthquakes.

## Honest limits (stated up front)

A flag is a statistical deviation, not a confirmed disaster. The baseline is cross-sensor. One deep-dive site means modest generalization claims, with an optional second site to show transfer. Kuttanad's "normal" includes human water management, which we model rather than ignore.

## Closing line

> Earth never stops changing. CounterSAR helps you tell the rhythm from the surprise.

Downloads take too long: what to do

The main trick is don’t download big files. Make them small first.

Use Google Earth Engine for almost everything. Sentinel-1, rainfall, soil moisture, elevation, land cover and Sentinel-2 all live there. Earth Engine does the cropping and resizing on Google’s computers, and you only export a tiny result.
Keep the area small. Use only the 20 × 20 km Kuttanad box, not the whole of Kerala.
Coarsen the resolution. Export at 100 m instead of the original 10 m. Files become about 100 times smaller.
Use fewer images. Use one orbit and one polarization pair, not everything. Weekly or 12-day data is enough.
Export in yearly chunks and run them at the same time. Start the exports first, then work on other things while they run.
For NISAR, download only the few scenes over the box. Choose the lightweight processed product (GCOV) rather than raw data, and start downloads immediately in the background. Do not wait for them. If the full scenes are huge, download one scene first, crop to the box, and delete the rest.
Do not download optical imagery or flood maps you don’t need. One or two clear Sentinel-2 images is enough for context.
Have a plan B ready. If NISAR is too slow or sparse, use two or three scenes only. The project still works: Sentinel-1 gives the long history, NISAR is the “now” demo.
Download over the best internet, and share. One person downloads a file and shares it with the team through Google Drive, so nobody downloads the same thing twice.

The simple rule

Start every export or download at once, then work on something else while it runs. Waiting idle is the biggest time loss.

Analyze NISAR data with different polarizations and/or different frequencies (from other sensors). Download data from different dates to show a time series and ancillary datasets (population, vegetation type, etc.) to contextualize the study area.
